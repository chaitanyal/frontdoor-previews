# Ned Kletz interview analysis for FrontDoor Health

Dr. Kletz has shut down the side practice, according to Chaitanya's clarification after the call. His account is historical customer research, and the closure reason and date were not provided. Ned's account supports testing a practice launch service that helps a clinician get established online, choose tools, and find initial patients. His own small side practice had little need for continued growth or an elaborate website once directories and referrals supplied enough patients. The website still mattered: almost all his patients said they had seen it, and it supported direct booking. Launch uncertainty, credibility, and administrative time are the strongest opportunities in this interview.

## Practice profile

Ned described a solo, cash-pay, entirely telehealth outpatient psychiatry practice operated alongside a full-time hospital job. His reasons were to continue outpatient work after leaving an outpatient setting and supplement his income. He deliberately kept the practice small: **thirty total patients**, with different visit frequencies, rather than thirty patients each month. His hospital schedule allowed flexible outpatient appointment times; he did not describe dedicating one or two full days each week to the practice. He reported charging **$800 an hour** in the WebMD discussion. [00:20, 06:13, 09:25, 11:47–13:09]

This is a useful example of a clinician seeking supplemental income with low overhead. It is a limited proxy for a clinician building a full-time or multi-provider practice, a distinction Ned repeatedly raised himself. His comments describe his past experience. Chaitanya subsequently clarified that this side practice has shut down; the call does not establish a launch date or why it closed. The closure should not be attributed to acquisition, website, or operational problems without further evidence. [18:04, 20:40, 27:04]

The remaining setup involved a Squarespace website built by his wife, an EMR borrowed from an associated practice with its permission, direct booking through Acuity Scheduling, payments he recalled as being through Squarespace, and communications through Spruce Care. The strongest missing resource was someone to explain the process. Ned explicitly said he had wanted someone like Chaitanya to direct him when getting started and had not had that person. This supports a launch-guidance offer, although it does not establish what he would have paid for it. [21:44–22:08]

## Website and online presence

His website had two distinct jobs: help prospective patients check him out and let them book. He said almost all his patients had seen it after finding him on Psychology Today, WebMD, or Zocdoc. Chaitanya described this as a credibility signal, and Ned agreed. That supports the website as an important step after discovery; the interview does not establish a measured conversion lift or how many patients came through organic website traffic. [16:41–18:04]

Ned considered a basic site sufficient for his side practice because demand was high and he wanted few patients. He said he would want a nicer site that drove business if the practice were his primary focus. His view that older patients were less deterred by the site's appearance is his interpretation, not evidence of a general age-based design preference. [06:51–07:55, 17:04]

For FrontDoor Health, the useful value proposition is a credible presence with clear information and working booking, aligned with the clinician's growth goal. This interview gives weaker support to selling aesthetic modernization alone to a practice already receiving enough patients. Chaitanya's account of three Austin practices that viewed previews without responding reinforces that hypothesis, but those outreach observations are Chaitanya's evidence, not Ned's buying behavior. ## Patient acquisition

Ned did not bring a patient panel from the associated practice. He built demand through directory listings and a local referral relationship. WebMD supplied the most business among the directories he discussed; Psychology Today was another useful source; Zocdoc produced relatively little. Exact patient counts, channel shares, and retention were not provided. [06:47, 07:55–10:20]

For referrals, he Googled nearby psychiatrists and called one. That psychiatrist had more patients than he could handle and began referring many patients. Ned stopped further outreach because he did not want that much demand. This is strong evidence that one relationship worked for him, not a quantified, repeatable acquisition guarantee. [10:42]

The online journey he described was **directory discovery → practice website → direct booking through Acuity → notification and appointment**. The interview does not establish that every patient followed every step, or attribute the referral patients to this same journey. [16:41–18:04]

The lesson for FrontDoor Health is to ask which channel needs help. A launch customer may need initial visibility and referrals; a practice with enough demand may care more about appropriate inquiries, patient fit, and fewer administrative interruptions. Buying entity-registration data was Chaitanya's proposed targeting idea, not a channel Ned had tested or endorsed as effective. [22:10]

Ned handled scheduling, emails, and calls himself. Acuity enabled direct booking and notifications, but did not eliminate administrative work. He initially offered free fifteen-minute consultations to assess fit, then stopped them because of the unpaid time. He sometimes received forms and entered the appointment without a prior consultation. [05:33, 13:52, 17:54–18:04, 24:34]

When Chaitanya proposed using a website or intake form to screen patients, Ned said forms were helpful to some extent but an interview could still be necessary for discerning patient selection. Treat this as a limit on automatic screening: the call supports reducing avoidable conversations, not replacing clinical judgment with a form. [14:20–15:08]

Ned valued professional presentation beyond the website: an assistant, credit-card payments rather than Venmo, and separate communications. Spruce's separate line kept patients from needing his personal number. Refills, side effects, and short clinical questions were recurring communication categories. A website service can make booking and contact paths clear; any deeper support for these messages would need a defined administrative role and clinician escalation. [05:33, 15:54–16:19, 24:01–26:45]

## Willingness to pay

The clearest demonstrated spending was on patient acquisition. Ned recalled about $200 per month for WebMD and said it was worthwhile against his $800 hourly rate: one patient a month more than covered it, and he reported multiple patients a month. This is his gross-revenue comparison, not a measured profit or lifetime-value calculation. The call does not specify appointment lengths or what each referred patient actually paid. He also described a low monthly listing fee for Psychology Today and a per-booking fee for Zocdoc as worthwhile. [07:55–10:20]

At the same time, he avoided office rent, paid staffing, and unpaid consultation time. His wife built the site. He recognized the value of help but accepted doing tasks himself to control overhead. That combination suggests price sensitivity for general convenience and greater willingness to spend when he could connect a cost to business generated. [05:33–06:25, 13:52, 24:01]

**No FrontDoor Health price was offered or accepted, no website budget was stated, and no purchase commitment was made.** His statement that launch guidance would have been helpful is retrospective interest. His comment that a full-time practice would warrant a nicer website is conditional interest. Neither establishes a price ceiling or willingness to pay FrontDoor Health $200 per month. [06:51, 21:44]

A useful next pricing test is a concrete launch package with defined deliverables and a real price, discussed with a clinician currently launching. For administrative support, first ask how much time it consumes and what the clinician already spends to address it.

## Product implications

1. **Test a practice launch offer first.** A proposed starting scope is launch guidance, a credible website, booking setup, directory readiness, and setup of contact paths to existing tools. The timing advantage is a hypothesis supported by Ned's retrospective need; it still requires interviews and paid tests with people currently starting practices. Legal formation can be an introduction to an appropriate professional rather than a promised outcome from this interview.
2. **Qualify the clinician's goals and capacity.** Ask whether this is supplemental income, a full-time practice, or a growth plan; the desired patient load; and the acquisition gap. Ned's low desired volume made additional marketing and overhead less attractive.
3. **Preserve and connect working tools.** His website, booking, communications, and EMR were separate resources. The first offer can make their patient-facing paths coherent. This call does not justify building an EMR or replacing his communication tools.
4. **Sell practical outcomes.** Test whether clinicians value being ready to launch, receiving suitable inquiries, enabling direct booking, and saving measurable setup time. A modern appearance can support credibility, but Ned's experience does not validate appearance as an urgent standalone purchase.
5. **Explore administration as a separate opportunity.** The discomfort is explicit, but budget and workload are unknown. Refills and side effects also make it broader than simple appointment handling. Validate frequency, response expectations, existing workflows, and a paid scope before expanding the website offer.
6. **Test trusted introductions as a route to customers.** His lawyer and technology choices came through friends or his wife. Ned offered to ask a friend who was building a larger practice about marketing. Warm introductions and launch-related professional relationships are reasonable hypotheses; entity-registration feeds remain unvalidated.

## Follow up research

Because Ned's side practice has shut down, treat him as a research contact and possible source of introductions rather than an active practice prospect. Ned offered to tell a close friend about Chaitanya and ask what he was doing for marketing. Chaitanya offered to send a short blurb that Ned could forward. The call records an offered introduction, not a completed introduction or an authorized follow-up message from this analysis. [27:43–28:29]

For the next interview, prioritize these questions:

- What is the launch date, intended patient volume, and primary income goal? Which setup decisions are still unresolved?
- Which task has delayed launch most, how long has it taken, and who has helped?
- What are actual setup, website, and marketing budgets? Would the clinician purchase a defined FrontDoor Health package at a stated price?
- How many inquiries, booked first appointments, and retained patients come from each source? Which source produces the best fit?
- How much weekly time goes to scheduling, screening, communications, and payments? Which tasks can an assistant handle, and which require the clinician?
- Which EMR was used, what specifically is frustrating about DrChrono, and what tool connections are required?
- What payment system and communication configuration were actually used? These details are not settled by Ned's recollection.

A full-time practice founder would help test whether the launch and growth problems are more urgent than they were in Ned's deliberately small side practice.
